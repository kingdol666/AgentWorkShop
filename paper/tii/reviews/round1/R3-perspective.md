# R3 — Perspective Review (Human–Automation Interaction, Safety Engineering, HITL Authority Design, Practical Deployability)

**Manuscript:** *AgentWorkShop: A Node-Native Integration Framework for LLM Multi-Agent Closed-Loop Control of Heterogeneous Industrial Production Lines*
**Venue:** IEEE Transactions on Industrial Informatics (TII)
**Reviewer seat:** R3 — human factors / operations management lens. Read-only review; no manuscript files were modified.

---

## 1. Mental-Model Reconstruction (mandatory comprehension test)

An operator's day with this system deployed runs through a web console or TUI workbench that share **one approval queue**. Once per device, an engineer: exports the device's integration descriptor (driver configuration is derived from it), tests connectivity, creates the node with a **semantic card** (unit, hard range S(n), recipe window W_r(p), warn band, freshness, a 2%-of-span step rule), provisions line/product/recipe, and **binds** the node *manual*, *auto*, or *unbound*. In operation, an agent team — a lead running a 1 s supervision tick with a deterministic rule-engine fallback, dispatching worker agents — *observes* via `daq_query`, *proposes* via `dcw_control`, which opens an optimization record freezing the pre-write baseline (previous value + 600 s statistics). On a **manual** binding, the proposal halts at stage 0: the operator sees a deduplicated approval request; nothing executes until a human decides; unapproved requests **lapse** with their record; and approval is **decoupled from execution** — every pipeline stage re-runs after the decision, defeating stale approvals. On **auto**, writes pass the batch-scoped recipe-window interlock, serialize under guardrails (one open record per agent, 30-min staleness, 300 s same-direction cooldown), are written with readback verification (Eq. 2), and land in a signed journal anchor with prev/new/source/actor. A **backstop** re-scores open auto records every 30 s after 120 s; three out-of-window samples trigger rollback, capped at K=2 per node lifetime, then mandatory human escalation. `dcw_judge` can return *keep/rollback/uncertain* but never actuates; alarm acknowledgment stays human with 15-min escalation. The human sits either as gatekeeper (manual), as escalation endpoint (auto, at K), or is structurally bypassed (unbound = no write path exists at all).

**Comprehension breakdown points — where an operator or plant manager could NOT know what the system would do:**

- **B1. Approval-request content is never specified.** What does the operator see when a request arrives — the proposed value against the current PV, window position, step size vs. the 2% rule, the agent's rationale, record/baseline statistics? The paper specifies what is *journalled after* the decision, never what is *shown before* it (Sec. IV-G).
- **B2. Lapse has no stated timer.** "Unapproved requests lapse with their record" (Sec. IV-G, Algorithm 1 line 2) — after how long? What does the UI show for a lapsed request? Does the agent re-propose afterwards (see M3)?
- **B3. K=2 is monotone per node lifetime with no reset policy.** A plant manager cannot know the steady-state consequence: after two lifetime automatic rollbacks, is the node effectively demoted to permanent human escalation? Reset on maintenance? On recipe change? Unstated (Sec. IV-E).
- **B4. Who may flip manual→auto, and is the binding act itself governed?** Choosing auto removes the human from that node's loop — the most consequential authorization in the system — yet no approval/journaling requirement for binding changes is stated (Sec. IV-G).
- **B5. Multi-operator semantics of the shared queue are undefined** (decision races, roles, two-person rules) and no shift-handover behavior for pending approvals or open records exists.
- **B6. No operator-facing "suspend the agents" control is described.** Gateway pause exists but is framed as platform governance, not as a human e-stop for a team; an operator cannot know from the paper whether/how they halt a misbehaving team across all its nodes.
- **B7. The `dcw_judge` "uncertain" verdict has no specified consumer** — who is expected to act on it?

## 2. Perspective Strengths

1. **The binding triad is a clean, checkable authority model.** *Unbound = the tool surface never dispatches a write* is the correct default-deny posture; *manual* and *auto* are distinguishable postures a plant safety engineer can reason about, and the paper is right that authority is "a property of the binding model, not of a prompt."
2. **Approval decoupled from execution with full stage re-run** (Sec. IV-G) is a genuinely well-reasoned design point — it closes the stale-approval/time-of-check-to-time-of-use hole that almost no HITL-LLM paper even names. Lapse-with-record semantics correctly prevents zombie approvals and unbounded queue growth.
3. **Judgment-not-execution separation** (`dcw_judge` returns verdicts but cannot actuate; rollbacks are separately invoked and re-traverse the full pipeline), plus **lead-only tools behind approval** and a **lead-gated cross-channel bus**, mirrors how authority actually works in human plant organizations.
4. **Attribution parity**: human approval decisions land in the same signed journal anchor as machine actions — the audit story treats human and agent decisions identically. This is the right foundation for post-incident review.
5. **Honest threat model, unusual candor**: harness engines are acknowledged as privileged local processes that could bypass the sanctioned path; indirect prompt injection is named with its actual failure mode; the zoning trade-off ("trading zoning isolation for in-process enforceability within one zone," Sec. IV-G) is stated as a trade-off; the invariants are explicitly "engineering properties, not certified safety functions" and the framework "does not replace certified instrumentation" (Sec. VI-A). IEC 61511 basic-control/SIS separation and IEC 62443 zoning are used correctly as boundaries, not as borrowed authority.
6. **Fail-safe directions are consistently chosen**: paused gateway defers rollbacks; governance unavailable exactly when actuation is unavailable; F6 storage-degradation fail-safe; alarm acknowledgment and escalation-at-K remain human steps.
7. **The negative LLM result is framed responsibly**: "the outcome is unfavorable to the agent," "a measured negative, not a headline" (Secs. V-H, V-I). In an operations culture where negative results are routinely buried, this is commendable and it directly motivates the propose/dispose posture rather than being explained away.

## 3. Major Issues

### M1. The approval gate's human-facing surface is unspecified — the operator cannot see *why* (Sec. IV-G; Figs. 1–2)
**What:** The paper's central safety mechanism is a human decision, yet the decision information is never described. The journal anchor records prev/new/source/actor *after* the fact; nothing specifies the *approval card*: proposed value vs. live PV, position within W_r(p), step size relative to the 2% span rule, the frozen baseline statistics, freshness, or the agent's rationale. Trust calibration is impossible if the approver sees only "agent X proposes 185 °C — approve?"
**Why it matters:** For a plant operator, an unexplained setpoint move from a stochastic system is exactly the condition that produces either blanket rejection (system unusable) or rubber-stamping (gate useless). Both failure modes destroy the manual mode's value, and neither is detectable from the paper's evaluation.
**Fix:** Specify the approval card contents (I would expect: proposal, PV, window and step-rule context, baseline stats, rationale excerpt, record identity, age) in Sec. IV-G, and connect it to the explanation/trust-calibration literature (Lee & See 2004; Parasuraman & Manzey 2010). If the web console already renders this, one figure of the walkthrough artifact would settle it.

### M2. Every human-facing number comes from a scripted bot; human-loop metrics are defined but never measured (Secs. V-A, V-E, V-H)
**What:** "Approval wait time" and "human interventions per task" are defined metric families (Sec. V-A), but the only reported approval-decision figure — 6–468 ms — is from a *scripted operator emulation* (Sec. V-E), and the L4 campaigns' "manual-mode bindings" were approved by the same emulation, which the authors honestly call "an autonomous-campaign configuration, not a production posture" (Sec. V-H). Meanwhile Sec. IV-G claims the gate is "engineered against the known failure modes of human-in-the-loop supervision [16]" with a single citation and no failure-mode → design-feature → residual-risk mapping.
**Why it matters:** The paper separates human response time from system latency precisely so as not to conflate them — but then never characterizes the human side at all. The claim of HITL failure-mode engineering is currently unsupported by either measurement or explicit argument.
**Fix:** Either (a) run even a small human-subject walkthrough (n≈5–8 operators, released walkthrough artifact, measure approval decision times and comprehension), or (b) explicitly scope human-loop performance as future work and rewrite the "engineered against the known failure modes" sentence into a concrete table: failure mode (automation bias, complacency, out-of-the-loop) → mechanism (lapse, dedup, decoupled re-run, acknowledgment) → residual risk. Option (b) is acceptable for TII; the current middle position is not.

### M3. Approval-load, alert fatigue, and lapse dynamics are unanalyzed (Sec. IV-G)
**What:** Requests are deduplicated per node and record, but a lead-supervised team can hold records across many nodes; nothing bounds approval requests per operator per hour, defines queue priority or aging, or states what the agent does after a lapse. If the agent re-proposes after a lapse, manual mode can produce a propose→lapse→re-propose request storm; if it does not re-propose, a distracted operator silently kills the optimization with no feedback loop — and repeated lapses train the workforce that requests can be ignored.
**Why it matters:** An approval queue is functionally a new **alarm class**. The paper cites ISA-18.2 for the alarm chain (Sec. II-A) yet never invokes alarm-management thinking (rationalization, operator-load budgets, cf. EEMUA 191) for the queue sitting next to it. Alert fatigue is the single most cited failure mode of supervisory systems and this design adds a new alert source.
**Fix:** State the lapse timer; state agent re-proposal/backoff behavior after lapse; give a worst-case requests/hour bound per operator for the released campaigns (the write ledgers — 7/7/15/10 writes per campaign — make this easy to estimate); and add queue-priority/aging semantics. One paragraph connecting approval flood to ISA-18.2/EEMUA practice would materially strengthen the safety-engineering story.

### M4. Authority administration is missing: binding governance, K-counter reset, multi-operator conflicts, shift handover (Sec. IV-G)
**What:** (a) "An engineer binds nodes" — with whose authorization? Binding changes (especially manual→auto, which removes the human from the loop) should be journaled, approved events akin to management-of-change; the paper is silent. (b) The K=2 counter is monotone per node lifetime with no reset policy (maintenance? recipe change? engineering override?) — over a multi-year node life this either permanently disables auto rollback or invites undocumented resets. (c) The shared queue specifies no roles, no two-person rule for high-consequence nodes, no arbitration of contradictory decisions, no handover semantics for pending approvals and open records across shifts.
**Why it matters:** These are the questions a plant manager asks first and the paper answers none of them: *who* may grant autonomy, *what happens* at 3 a.m. when the approver's shift ends with a pending request, and *whether* the escalation path degrades permanently after K=2. Real deployments fail on administration, not on pipeline mechanics.
**Fix:** Make binding changes first-class journaled, approval-gated events; state a counter-reset policy (or explicitly say K is lifetime and auto-mode retirement is the consequence); add a minimal role model (commissioning engineer / shift operator / supervisor) and handover semantics (do pending approvals survive shift end, or lapse; are open records reassigned).

### M5. "Commissioning is configuration, not code" is half-operationalized for a practitioner (Secs. I, III-A, V-B)
**What:** The measured portability result (five devices, five drivers, ~16 s, identical structural verdicts) is strong *for the driver-config derivation*, and the five-step flow is concrete. But the expensive parts of real commissioning are not measured: (a) authoring the semantic card — hard range, recipe window, warn band — is process-engineering expert content with no stated source, validation workflow, or owner; (b) a *wrong* recipe window produces either false blocks (operator loses trust immediately) or accepted bad writes (the 0% false-block evidence is measured only against self-defined windows); (c) real vendor integration descriptors vary wildly in quality; the one real-device tier (L3, Table I) exercises latency, not commissioning effort.
**Why it matters:** TII's practitioner readers will quote "16 s, zero code" and be misled unless the human-authored, expert-validated configuration surface is costed. A wrong window is also a *safety* configuration error, not just an integration cost.
**Fix:** Report engineer-hours for the one real-device integration (even a coarse estimate from the L3 tier), enumerate the per-device human-authored artifacts and their authors (instrument engineer? process engineer?), and describe (or commit to) a window-validation workflow (e.g., window values must be traced to the recipe specification / process-safety documentation).

## 4. Minor Issues

1. **m1 (negative-result framing, Sec. V-H).** Mostly honest and commendable, but the 0.35 campaign is confounded: its MQTT thickness channel *refused connection*, so it is partly an infrastructure failure, not pure decision quality. The median 0.76 (of 0.75/0.76/0.76) is the cleaner statistic — state the per-campaign cause attribution explicitly. Also, n=4 campaigns, one seed, one engine (glm-5.3-flash), pre-discipline sampling settings: "propose/dispose" is a *design hypothesis supported by this evidence*, not a demonstrated necessity. The paper mostly words it carefully ("motivates the framework's propose/dispose design"); keep it that way and avoid any causal strengthening in the abstract/conclusion.
2. **m2 (`dcw_judge` uncertain, Sec. IV-E).** The *uncertain* verdict has no specified consumer or operator action. One sentence needed.
3. **m3 (levels-of-automation grounding, Secs. II-B, IV-G).** The entire HITL design leans on one 2000 citation. The *irony of automation* (Bainbridge 1983) is directly on point for the auto posture: the more reliable auto mode becomes, the less vigilant the approver. Out-of-the-loop performance (Metzger & Parasuraman 2001), trust calibration (Lee & See 2004), and automation bias (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010) belong here.
4. **m4 (broader implications, Sec. VI).** Within scope, the paper is candid about not replacing certified instrumentation, but says nothing about workforce/role implications (the operator becomes an exception-handler for LLM proposals), approval rubber-stamping as a safety-culture risk, or the regulatory path (who signs off an LLM-proposed setpoint under management-of-change; how the framework's journal maps to audit expectations). Two or three sentences in Sec. VI-A would do; for TII's industrial audience their absence is conspicuous.
5. **m5 (backstop as de facto protection layer, Secs. IV-E, VI-A).** K=2 and B=3 are engineering constants with no integrity claim — correctly, the paper disclaims certified-safety status. A sentence relating the backstop to layers-of-protection-analysis / independent-protection-layer thinking would pre-empt reviewers or adopters treating the backstop as a safety case.
6. **m6 (interlock bypass management).** It is good practice that the hard range is structurally unbypassable and the bench bypass is build-gated ("production builds refuse the flag," Sec. V-G). State explicitly that *no operator-level interlock bypass exists by design* — bypass registers are a real-plant concern and their absence is a feature worth advertising.
7. **m7 (twin avatar, Sec. IV-D).** The device-twin's "virtual operator avatar" showing "the agent in charge" is a genuinely good who-is-acting affordance; it is presented but never connected to situation awareness or to the approval workflow. One sentence on its role would help.
8. **m8 (timing semantics).** Alarm-acknowledgment escalation defaults to 15 min; approvals lapse on an unstated timer; records go stale at 30 min. Unify and tabulate the human-side timing constants (lapse, escalation, staleness, cooldown, K, B) in one table — Sec. IV-G is currently the only place an operator would have to reconstruct them from.
9. **m9 (presentation).** Fig. 1's caption is a paragraph-long specification; the walkthrough artifact is referenced (Sec. V-B) but not shown in the paper. Given the HITL gate is the human product, one screenshot of the approval queue/card would communicate more than a page of prose.

## 5. Recommendation

**Major Revision.**

**Rationale.** From the human-automation-interaction seat, this is one of the more carefully *reasoned* HITL authority designs in the LLM-industrial literature: the manual/auto/unbound binding triad with default-deny, approval decoupled from execution with full stage re-run, lapse-with-record semantics, judgment-not-execution, lead-only tools behind approval, and attribution parity between human and machine decisions together form a coherent authority architecture, and the paper's candor about its threat model (privileged harness engines, prompt injection, the zoning trade-off, "not certified safety functions") and about its negative LLM result is exemplary. But the *human* side of the human-in-the-loop gate is underspecified at every point where a real operator would touch it: what approvers see (M1), how loaded they get and what lapse does (M3), who administers authority and what happens across shifts (M4), and every human-facing number in the evaluation comes from a scripted bot (M2), leaving the "engineered against known HITL failure modes" claim unproved. The deployability claim is half-measured (M5). None of these require new science — they require specification, a small human walkthrough or an honest scoping statement, and a few paragraphs connecting the approval queue to alarm-management and authority-administration practice. The gap between the mechanism design's quality and the human-factors treatment's depth is exactly what a major revision is for; the core contribution is sound and worth publishing once the human boundary is specified with the same rigor as the write path.
