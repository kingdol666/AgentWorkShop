D. Approval-Context Guard and Ablation
The production handler blocked all 12 one-field mutations
before a mock write or execution record. The queued context
change was also refused before recipe_apply dispatch.
In the ablation, removing the tuple comparison allowed six
product/run changes through; each produced two mock writes
and an execution record, while the other checks still blocked
line/recipe changes. The local unit suite passed 566/566 checks
across seven suites.
The ablation isolates the tuple comparison within the tested
software state. It does not demonstrate atomicity during net-
work or device I/O, safe physical operation, operator effec-
tiveness, or end-to-end DAQ integrity.
E. Archived Four-Scenario Control Sequences and Outcomes
The current route study collected one post-trial process
sample per task. Fig. 5 instead shows four archived seed-
42 simulator missions from an earlier scripted direct-node
interface. Each column pairs requested setpoint changes with
process-variable (PV) observations and its own target window;
event indices give order, not elapsed time.
In injection molding, hold pressure and time increased from
45 to 58.2 bar and 6 to 10.64 s; measured weight rose from
31.25 to 32.17 g, ending 0.02 g inside the 32.5±0.35 g window.
Five wastewater blower increments from 26 to 34 Hz raised
dissolved oxygen (DO) from 0.214 to 3.517 mg/L, inside
the 3.4 ± 0.6 mg/L window; later NaOH and PAC actions
addressed separate pH and phosphorus stages. In annealing,
zone-3 temperature requests rose from 730 to 850◦C; later
zone-2 and speed changes produced 97.88–99.84 HV, and the
terminal value was 100.08 HV in the 95 ± 6 HV window.
The 101.3 HV value after a reverted 176 m/min candidate
appears only in event text. For BOPET, casting speed, fast-
roll speed, and rail width changed from 32 to 33.8 m/min,
118 to 120.3 m/min, and 3000 to 3035 mm; thickness fell from
28.10 to 25.66 µm, ending 0.04 µm inside the upper edge of
its 25.0 ± 0.7 µm window. There is one historical trajectory
per scenario; the records illustrate scenario-specific feedback
but not repeatability or current-route attainment.
F. Evidence Synthesis
The route study supports record continuity for the tested
task and direct-call contracts across two preconfigured map-
pings; all twelve route-linked samples were below target. The
separate onboarding replay passed the documented C01 con-
figuration checks with isolated online MQTT/Timescale and
one simulator- confirmed write. The mock-state comparison
shows that the tested context check blocked stale requests
before software dispatch. Fig. 5 is historical evidence and is
not pooled with these results.
Together, these results distinguish command completion
from process outcome: a verified readback confirms the set-
point path, whereas the later run-tagged sample indicates
whether the process variable entered its target band. The
study establishes selected integration behavior, one simulated
onboarding instance, and a bounded software guard; it does
not establish process improvement, general portability, or
physical-plant performance. Supplementary File S1 includes

VI. DISCUSSION
This section reads the results against the design contract.
It first interprets what the tested routes and the onboarding
replay support, then states the scope that the evidence does
not cover.
A. Interpretation
AgentWorkShop composes collaborative-task services, pro-
duction context, named operations, semantic signal access,
configured device drivers, and service-owned records into a
shared task-to-device path. The current experiments trace this
design through task or direct-operation entry, approval-bound
dispatch, readback receipt, and a later run-tagged process sam-
ple. Registered task routes preserve the task, approval, receipt,
and sample associations; direct REST preserves operation,
readback, and sample records without a task edge. The two
simulator scenes use different signal and protocol mappings,
so the observed joins support the tested composition and route-
specific record contract.
The v0.8.3 onboarding replay answers a different question
from the route runs: it exercises protocol access, heterogeneous
MES data, channel construction, acquisition, and an approved
write inside one workflow, which single operation calls cannot
show. Because the workflow was not repeated under a changed
profile or binding set, it supports workflow execution for the
documented blueprint while leaving configuration reuse open.
A replay with a pinned revision and a changed binding set
would separate those two readings.
The process results bound the interpretation of that ev-
idence. Cast-film samples range from 45.69 to 46.69 µm,
or 1.31–2.31 µm below the 48 µm lower bound. Annealing
samples range from 674.40 to 679.60 ◦C, or 20.40–25.60 ◦C
below the 700 ◦C lower bound. These measurements are
single snapshots taken 20 s after an operation, so they cannot
establish a response curve, convergence, or the cause of the
observed shortfall.
Fig. 5 pairs requested setpoint changes with the result-
ing process observations in four separate archived seed-42
simulator missions. The trajectories show scenario-specific
responses, including the wastewater mission’s later pH and
total-phosphorus actions and the annealing candidate that
was reverted. Their terminal observations lie within their
own target windows, but each panel contains one historical
scripted direct-node trajectory. The figure therefore explains
the structure of those archived action–response records; it does
not establish target attainment by current v0.8.0 routes or
repeatability across runs.
This gap between command evidence and process evidence
is central to the framework’s execution model. A verified
readback confirms that the requested setpoint reached the
configured device; it does not show that the process variable
entered its target band. A task can therefore complete its
approved operation path while its associated process sample
remains out of band. Keeping the receipt and run-tagged
sample as separate, linked records lets the system report
both facts without treating command completion as product
attainment. The current results validate this distinction in the
tested software paths, not an improvement in control quality.
The route evidence also depends on each entry contract.
Registered model-mediated and fixture-backed CLI tasks re-
tain task, approval, receipt, and sample links. The fixed-call
replay exercises the registered harness with deterministic input
but does not assert task completion. Direct REST reaches the
same operation boundary and retains a verified receipt and run-
tagged sample without a task edge. The fixture replay, direct
REST, and CLI checks ran in separate isolated environments;
the model-mediated triplets shared a persistent simulator per
scene. These path-specific contexts and unequal sample counts
mean their numerical similarity is not evidence of route
equivalence. All route-study runs used the SQLite fallback
because MQTT and Timescale were unavailable; the separate
C01 onboarding replay exercised online MQTT/Timescale on
isolated services. The results support the specified record
contracts under two configured signal and protocol mappings
and feasibility of one blueprint-driven simulated onboarding
workflow. They do not establish interchangeable adapters,
configuration reuse, or general portability to an unseen scene.
The context-guard test identifies the software check’s con-
tribution under mock state: comparing the approved line–
product–recipe–run tuple blocked product/run mutations that
the retained checks alone did not reject. This supports a
bounded claim about software dispatch. It does not show atom-
icity across network or device I/O, safe physical operation,
or operator effectiveness. Synthetic approval also does not
measure human review performance [25]–[27].
B. Scope and Limitations
Only the reported adapter and scene configurations were
exercised. The single C01 replay does not test repeatable
onboarding, profile or binding reuse across lines, arbitrary
unseen-scene configuration, or physical transfer, and it ran on a
working-tree build whose source commit was not pinned. The
evaluation also does not establish general adapter interchange-
ability, normal online DAQ operation in the route trials, or a
platform baseline. The feature-level related-work comparison
positions the implementation question; it does not show that
AgentWorkShop is more efficient or more capable than another
platform. A comparative study would need a shared task set,
explicit integration-effort or record-completeness measures,
and matched deployments.
The route study used one simulator instance per scene,
three sequential model-mediated trials per scene, and one
post-trial process sample per trial. These samples are not
independent replicates and cannot establish a process response
curve. Follow-up evaluation should vary configuration profiles
under a controlled onboarding protocol, use repeated process
trials, and compare against a defined alternative integration
path. Operation restrictions do not establish security [28]–[30]
or replace independent PLC/SIS safeguards [31].

VII. CONCLUSION
AgentWorkShop brings task adapters, production context,
named operations, semantic I/O, configured protocol drivers,
and service-owned records into a modular task-to-device
framework. Tested task routes and direct calls used a shared
operation boundary across cast-film and annealing simulator
mappings while preserving route-specific records. A sepa-
rate v0.8.3 replay completed one documented coating-line
onboarding configuration with online DAQ checks and a
simulator-confirmed smoke write. Route-study samples re-
mained below target, and a mock-state ablation isolated the
effect of an approval-bound context check. The study estab-
lishes selected integration, one simulated onboarding instance,
and software-contract behavior; it does not establish process
improvement, configuration reuse, physical transfer, general
portability, or comparative platform benefit.
