\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
The evaluation comprised three primary blocks and separate route checks. Scene--protocol probes tested whether selected mappings accepted admissible values, rejected structural-range requests, and returned matching readbacks where available. Five-seed simulator missions recorded target-band endpoints under a deterministic scripted policy and a no-write condition. A mock-driver ablation tested whether the process-window check blocked selected excursions before dispatch. Approval and authorization paths were checked separately. Counts remain separate because the blocks use different units and fixtures: pipeline assertions, node requests, seed--scene endpoints, and guard injections are not pooled.

\subsection{Test Conditions and Counting Units}
\label{sec:eval-protocol}
Two integrated pipeline executions, documented in Supplementary File~S7, each reported 77 passing assertions, zero warnings, and zero failed assertions. The runner reused a platform and simulator that were already running. The manifests identify commit \texttt{da627d21} and record \texttt{agentHarness=(none)}; these executions therefore exercised configured service paths without an active language model or live harness. The 77 checks are assertions within each execution, not 77 independent trials. Supplementary File~S7 contains the run records and archived source summaries.

The scene--protocol probe records each requested value, expected decision, actual outcome, and available readback; its unit is one configured node request. For the optimization comparison, the unit is a seed--scene pair. C0 held each simulator for 60~s without writes, whereas C1 used a deterministic scripted policy through the governed-write path. Each C1 seed ran for 150.41--172.90~s. Because exposure time and mission logic differed, the comparison describes the tested conditions but does not isolate the effect of the write policy. The process-window ablation used three repetitions per arm. Each repetition included six negative probes, three nominal legal requests, and three boundary requests. Across the three repetitions, each arm therefore included 18 negative probes: six targeted window violations and 12 structural-range probes. The ablation ran in benchmark mode against a mock driver, so its outcomes assess the software interlock in that fixture rather than plant-level protection. Supplementary File~S7 preserves the source CSVs and per-run records.

\subsection{Configured Scene and Protocol Paths}
\label{sec:eval-mapping}
The retained raw probe run covers 55 configured writable nodes across four simulator scenes and five protocol families: Modbus over Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), Modbus remote terminal unit (RTU), OPC UA, MQTT, and Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP). Each scene used four of the five families. Table~\ref{tab:scene-probe-results} reports the node allocation by scene and separates accepted in-range and upper-boundary requests from structural negative probes. These counts describe configured mappings, not throughput or full scene--protocol matrix coverage. Readback consistency is reported only for accepted requests with an available actuator value. One biaxial-line structural case was not collected, and HTTP routes did not expose actuator readback. The evaluated subset therefore covers selected mappings; it does not establish general portability across scenes or protocols.

For each configured writable node, the expected-acceptance set comprised three interior values and the tested upper boundary; the structural set comprised four values outside the configured engineering range. Across the collected subset, all 220 admissible requests were accepted, all 219 recorded structural probes were rejected, and all 216 available actuator readbacks matched their requests. These counts characterize the configured examples; they do not estimate false-block rates or establish complete matrix coverage. In a separate configuration check, the integrated pipeline loaded a film-line preset with five endpoint types and rejected nine of nine selected negative probes without code changes. Because the runtime was reused, this was not an independent cold-start deployment.

\subsection{Target-Band Comparison}
\label{sec:eval-optimization}
The same five seeds, 42--46, were used in the injection, wastewater, and
annealing scenes. Both conditions began from their scenario blueprint and
seeded simulator state. C0 made no writes for 60~s; its endpoint was the mean
of the final five process samples. C1 ran a deterministic scenario mission
through the shared governed-write service, queried DAQ after configured
settling intervals, and applied scenario-specific guard or recovery logic.
The policy was scripted, not Agent-generated. C1 took 150.41--172.90~s per
seed, so the conditions differed in exposure time and mission logic.

We assessed each recorded terminal process-variable statistic against its
configured target band. For C0, that statistic is
the mean of the final five samples; for C1, it is the mission-terminal
reading. The runner's \texttt{attained} field is not comparable across
conditions because C0 combines the process-variable band with configured
guards, whereas C1 uses scenario-specific mission criteria. We therefore
recomputed band membership from the raw \texttt{pv}, \texttt{target}, and
\texttt{tol} columns.

For injection mass, the target was $32.5\pm0.35$~g. C0's terminal means were
31.206--31.272~g and C1's mission-terminal readings were 32.173--32.340~g,
placing 0/5 and 5/5 recorded statistics, respectively, inside the band. For
wastewater dissolved oxygen (DO), the target was $3.4\pm0.6$~mg/L; C0
terminal means were 0.198--0.268~mg/L and C1 readings were 3.576--3.864~mg/L,
again giving 0/5 versus 5/5 in-band statistics. In annealing, the target was $95\pm6$ on the Vickers hardness scale (HV); C0 terminal means were 134.36--134.90~HV and C1 readings were 97.44--99.90~HV. Across the 15 seed--scene endpoints, all 15 C1 readings and none of the 15 C0 terminal means were within their respective bands. Across the five C1 seeds, the scripted missions
issued 12 writes in 17 rounds for injection, 56 in 59 for wastewater, and 41
in 50 for annealing. These counts describe workload, not efficiency. Because
the endpoint conditions were not time-matched and used different mission
logic, the contrast is descriptive and does not compare the runtime with an
established controller. Fig.~\ref{fig:latest-evaluation} shows each endpoint
and its configured band.

\subsection{Process-Window Interlock Ablation}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}
The primary contrast was the full guard against the same configuration with only the process-window interlock removed. With the guard enabled, 18/18 negative probes were rejected before the mock driver and 0/6 targeted window violations reached it. Removing that interlock reduced the negative-probe rejections to 12/18: all six targeted window violations reached the driver, while the 12 out-of-range structural probes remained blocked. Each arm also accepted 9/9 nominal legal requests without a false block and met its configured boundary expectations in 9/9 checks.

The no-readback arm retained the process-window interlock and produced the same 18/18 rejection result. The combined no-window/no-readback arm matched the no-interlock arm on these stimuli. No readback-mismatch stimulus was included, so the experiment isolates the observed process-window effect but says nothing about readback-check effectiveness or interaction between the two checks. The implementation has separate production-mode tests for the interval, step, node-range, recipe-window, and hold-window guards; each configured case returned its expected rejection code, while the legal baseline was accepted. Those single-case checks are route demonstrations, not rate estimates.

\subsection{Approval and Authorization Path Checks}
\label{sec:eval-approval}
A separate four-call simulator pilot exercised the approval route. In manual mode, the scripted approval case changed the control value from 202 to 204.8, while the scripted rejection left it at 216. The two auto-mode calls also executed, but they began at values different from those in the manual-mode pairings defined by the runner. The decisions came from a test fixture, no operator made them, and the calls do not form a matched approval-on/off comparison. We therefore treat these records as a path check rather than an estimate of the approval effect. Supplementary File~S7 retains the values and decision trace.

A separate authorization check rejected one unbound write and one cross-node write, while accepting one write to a bound control node. Together, these examples showed that the configured checks returned their expected outcomes in the tested simulator routes. They do not establish a general safety rate, physical-equipment behavior, or live-Agent decision quality.
