\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The scene--protocol probes and simulator missions address different parts of the integration claim. The probes show that the selected configurations resolved to the intended node and driver paths. The missions show that a deterministic policy issued governed writes and returned process endpoints in the tested simulator scenes. The target-band contrast does not isolate the effect of the write path or establish control performance: the no-write and scripted conditions differed in duration, mission logic, and endpoint summaries. The planned scene--protocol matrix remains incomplete, and the film-line configuration check reused a running platform rather than testing a cold deployment.

The process-window ablation supports a narrower causal conclusion. Removing the check allowed all six targeted excursions to reach the mock driver while structural-range checks remained active. This isolates the process-window predicate in the tested fixture. The ablation ran in benchmark mode and included no readback-mismatch stimulus, so it does not assess the full production guard stack or readback-check effectiveness. Separate production-mode examples exercised the interval, step, node-range, recipe-window, and hold-window branches. These are route checks, not estimates of rejection rates under load.

The approval and harness claims are also bounded by the tests. The approval pilot used scripted decisions, included no operator interaction, and lacked matched approval-on/off conditions; it therefore cannot establish a human-in-the-loop interception effect. The runtime exposes a common operation contract to registered harness adapters, but no active Agent harness was used in these runs. Harness replacement and agent decision quality remain untested.

The evidence model also constrains how future deployments should be evaluated. A command receipt records the write outcome; an actuator readback reports the returned control value when available; and a DAQ sample records a later process measurement. The optimization path checks that its target DAQ binding is visible, but these tests do not verify that every configured observation is the commissioned response to the selected control point. The DAQ query uses a trailing 45-s window, so a shorter settling delay may leave pre-write samples in the result. Before using these measurements to assess a write, each scene needs a verified control--measurement pairing and observation delay.

The integrated-pipeline manifests identify the reported commit and show that the platform and simulator were already running. They do not capture a hermetic snapshot of all active service source. Supplementary File~S7 retains the runner inputs and records needed to audit the reported runs. Application-level checks supplement PLC, SIS, and equipment protection; the simulator and mock-driver tests do not establish physical-plant safety.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This work presents a configuration-driven runtime that binds production context and semantic signals to named operations, registered protocol drivers, and shared write governance. Across the collected four-scene simulator probes, 220/220 admissible requests were accepted, 219/219 collected structural-range requests were rejected, and 216/216 available actuator readbacks matched. In the three-scene mission comparison, 15/15 scripted-policy endpoints fell within their target bands, compared with 0/15 no-write endpoints; this descriptive contrast used different exposure times and mission logic. In the mock-driver ablation, the process-window check blocked all six targeted violations, whereas removing it forwarded all six while structural checks remained active. In these simulation and mock-driver settings, the results demonstrate the selected scene mappings, scripted-write path, and process-window guard. They do not establish live-Agent or harness behavior, human-approval effects, or physical-plant protection.
